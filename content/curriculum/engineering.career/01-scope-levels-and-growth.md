---
title: Scope, levels, and growth
minutes: 17
summary: What actually changes between levels isn't skill at writing code — it's the size and ambiguity of the problem you can be handed.
---

Ask most engineers what separates a senior engineer from a junior one, and
the first answer is usually "they're better at coding." That's true, but
it's not the thing that actually drives promotion, and treating it as the
whole answer is precisely what makes career growth feel confusing and
arbitrary — because the thing that's actually changing is scope, and it's
rarely stated that plainly.

## What "level" actually measures

```text
  the axis most career ladders are ACTUALLY built around,
  underneath the specific titles: how BIG and how AMBIGUOUS a
  problem can be handed to this person, and have them figure
  out not just HOW to solve it, but WHAT actually needs solving
  in the first place.

    JUNIOR       given a well-specified task ("implement this
                 exact function"), executes it correctly
    MID-LEVEL      given a FEATURE ("build checkout"), breaks it
                 down into tasks, handles the ambiguity WITHIN
                 that feature's own boundaries
    SENIOR           given a PROBLEM ("checkout conversion is
                 too low"), figures out what the actual
                 SOLUTION even should be — which might not
                 involve code at all
    STAFF+             given a DIRECTION ("we need to grow this
                 part of the business"), figures out WHAT
                 PROBLEMS are worth solving in the first place,
                 across a scope spanning multiple teams
```

```text
  → this is why "am I a good enough CODER to be promoted" is
    frequently the wrong question past a certain point — code
    quality is a genuine PREREQUISITE at every level (it never
    stops mattering), but it stops being the DIFFERENTIATOR
    between levels once it's already solid; the differentiator
    becomes how much AMBIGUITY someone can absorb and resolve
    on their own, without needing it handed to them
    pre-resolved.
```

## Scope grows in a specific, recognizable direction

```text
  the progression underneath every ladder's specific wording:

    IMPACT on ONE task → IMPACT on ONE feature → IMPACT on
    ONE team's roadmap → IMPACT across MULTIPLE teams →
    IMPACT on the ORGANIZATION's overall direction
```

```text
  → this is why "I write really good code" alone eventually
    STALLS as a promotion case past a certain level — it's
    necessary, but it doesn't by itself demonstrate GROWING
    SCOPE, and scope (not code quality in isolation) is what
    the higher levels of most ladders are actually measuring,
    explicitly or not.
```

## Promotion: evidence of ALREADY operating at the next level, not a reward for past work

```text
  the recurring, genuine misunderstanding: "I've worked really
  hard, I DESERVE a promotion" — hard work and LONGEVITY at
  the current level are not, by themselves, what most
  promotion processes actually evaluate.

  → the more accurate framing: promotion is usually a
    RECOGNITION that someone is ALREADY, DEMONSTRABLY operating
    at the NEXT level — not a reward that, once granted,
    CAUSES them to start operating at it. this reframes the
    actual, practical question from "have I put in enough time
    at this level" to "am I already taking on the SCOPE the
    next level actually requires, before anyone official grants
    me the title for it."
```

## Generalist vs. specialist: both real, legitimate paths

```text
  GENERALIST      broad competence across many areas — valuable
                 specifically for SCOPING and CONNECTING work
                 across boundaries, seeing how different pieces
                 fit together

  SPECIALIST         deep expertise in ONE area — valuable
                 specifically for the HARDEST problems within
                 that particular area, ones a generalist
                 genuinely couldn't solve without that depth
```

```text
  → neither path is objectively "more senior" than the other —
    they're DIFFERENT, both entirely legitimate shapes of
    genuine impact, and CHOOSING deliberately between them (or
    consciously choosing some blend of both) is a real,
    important career decision, not a mistake to be corrected by
    picking the "right" one — because there generally isn't a
    single right answer independent of what a specific person
    actually wants and where they're actually most valuable.
```

## The trap of chasing the title instead of the scope

```text
  → chasing a TITLE directly ("I want to be a Staff Engineer")
    without the underlying SCOPE actually growing first
    generally doesn't work, and even in the rare case where it
    technically succeeds, it tends to feel genuinely hollow —
    the title without the scope leaves someone without the
    actual DECISION-MAKING AMBIGUITY the level is supposed to
    represent, which is usually the thing that made the level
    feel worth wanting in the first place.
```

```text
  → the more reliable path, in practice: deliberately SEEK OUT
    growing scope and ambiguity FIRST (volunteer for the
    ambiguous, not-yet-well-defined problem nobody's claimed
    yet) — the title tends to follow the demonstrated scope,
    which is a genuinely more reliable, if less directly
    controllable, path than trying to negotiate or campaign for
    the title in isolation, disconnected from the underlying
    work.
```

## What to take away

1. What actually differentiates levels is the size and ambiguity of problem
   someone can be handed and resolve — not raw coding skill, which stays a
   prerequisite at every level without being the differentiator between
   them.
2. Scope grows in a specific, recognizable direction — from one task, to
   one feature, to a team's roadmap, to impact across multiple teams —
   which is why "I write really good code" alone eventually stalls as a
   promotion case.
3. Promotion recognizes someone already, demonstrably operating at the next
   level — it doesn't cause that operation to begin; the useful question is
   "am I already taking on that scope," not "have I put in enough time."
4. Generalist and specialist are both legitimate, equally senior paths —
   choosing between them deliberately is a real career decision, not a
   mistake correctable by picking the "right" one.
5. Chasing a title directly, without the underlying scope, tends to feel
   hollow even when it technically succeeds — seeking growing scope and
   ambiguity first is the more reliable path, with the title tending to
   follow.
