An interview is, underneath the conversation, a measurement instrument
trying to predict something genuinely hard to predict: how someone will
actually perform in a job, from a few hours of conversation. Most of what
makes interviewing hard is the same thing that makes any measurement hard —
noise, bias, and a signal that's easy to contaminate without realizing it.

## What an interview is actually trying to measure

```text
  → the genuine, structural DIFFICULTY: predicting REAL, ongoing
    job performance from a few HOURS of a fundamentally
    ARTIFICIAL, high-pressure conversation is a hard prediction
    problem on its face — this is worth naming explicitly, up
    front, because it's precisely why SIGNAL and NOISE (the
    Randomized and Probabilistic Algorithms chapter's
    vocabulary, resurfacing here in a very different context)
    are both genuinely, unavoidably present in every single
    interview's outcome, and the entire craft of interviewing
    well is maximizing the first while minimizing the second,
    never eliminating either one completely.
```

## Calibration: making sure "strong hire" means the same thing across interviewers

```text
  interviewer A's "strong hire"          might be interviewer
                                            B's "no hire" — for
                                            the EXACT SAME
                                            candidate, evaluated
                                            on the exact same
                                            performance

  → without EXPLICIT calibration, "strong hire" is a genuinely
    SUBJECTIVE, personal label that means something different
    depending entirely on which specific interviewer happens to
    be using it — this is exactly why structured, SHARED
    RUBRICS (specific, concrete, observable criteria, agreed on
    IN ADVANCE, before candidates are seen) matter: they align
    what "strong hire" ACTUALLY MEANS, concretely, across
    everyone using that same label independently.
```

```text
  → CALIBRATION SESSIONS (interviewers discussing PAST
    candidates together, comparing their independent scores
    against each other, and specifically discussing WHY any
    disagreements happened) are the practical mechanism that
    keeps a rubric's shared meaning from silently DRIFTING apart
    over time, across different interviewers, each one
    developing their own private, unstated interpretation.
```

## Structured vs. unstructured interviews

```text
  UNSTRUCTURED    every candidate gets a genuinely DIFFERENT
                 conversation, following whatever direction
                 happens to come up naturally — comfortable and
                 natural-feeling for the INTERVIEWER, but
                 genuinely hard to compare fairly ACROSS
                 different candidates who each had a
                 substantively different conversation

  STRUCTURED        every candidate gets asked the SAME core
                 questions, evaluated against the SAME explicit
                 rubric — a real, deliberate trade-off of some
                 conversational naturalness for the ability to
                 genuinely, fairly COMPARE across candidates
```

```text
  → research on interview VALIDITY (how well interview
    performance actually predicts real, later job performance)
    consistently favors structured interviews — an unstructured
    "vibe check," however confident and genuine it FEELS to the
    interviewer conducting it, correlates substantially WORSE
    with actual future job performance than a well-designed
    structured process does.
```

## The biases a loop has to be deliberately designed around

```text
  AFFINITY BIAS        favoring a candidate who reminds the
                       interviewer of THEMSELVES (similar
                       background, similar interests,
                       "culture fit" that's really just
                       "reminds me of me") — this is a real,
                       well-documented, genuinely pervasive
                       effect, not a rare or unusual failure

  ANCHORING               the FIRST interviewer's rating
                       (shared with the group before others
                       have independently weighed in)
                       disproportionately influences EVERY
                       subsequent interviewer's own rating —
                       even when each of them believes,
                       genuinely and sincerely, that their own
                       evaluation is entirely independent
```

```text
  → the practical, structural fixes: interviewers submitting
    scores INDEPENDENTLY, BEFORE seeing anyone else's rating
    (directly counters anchoring specifically), and a DIVERSE
    interview PANEL, deliberately, rather than a single
    homogeneous group whose members are all likely to share the
    exact same blind spots as each other — a loop's DESIGN has
    to account for these documented, real, well-studied human
    biases; simply asking individual interviewers to try harder
    to "just be objective" does not, in practice, actually work.
```

## What a good interview loop actually tests

```text
  → the practical goal for loop DESIGN: each individual
    interview should test something GENUINELY DIFFERENT (coding
    ability, system design judgment, how someone communicates
    and collaborates under real pressure) rather than several
    interviews redundantly re-testing the SAME thing under a
    different name — this maximizes the total, distinct SIGNAL
    actually gathered from a necessarily limited amount of a
    candidate's time, rather than wasting some of that scarce
    time re-confirming something an earlier interview in the
    SAME loop already established with reasonable confidence.
```

## What to take away

1. An interview is a measurement instrument with unavoidable signal and
   noise — the craft is maximizing the first and minimizing the second,
   never eliminating either entirely.
2. Without explicit calibration, "strong hire" means something genuinely
   different to different interviewers — shared rubrics and calibration
   sessions comparing past candidates keep that meaning from silently
   drifting apart across a team.
3. Structured interviews (same core questions, same explicit rubric)
   consistently predict future job performance better than an unstructured
   "vibe check," however confident it feels to the interviewer conducting it.
4. Affinity bias and anchoring are real, well-documented, pervasive
   effects — the fix is structural (independent scoring before seeing
   others' ratings, a deliberately diverse panel), not simply asking
   individuals to try harder to be objective.
5. A well-designed loop has each interview test something genuinely
   different, maximizing the total distinct signal gathered from a
   candidate's necessarily limited time rather than redundantly re-testing
   what an earlier interview already established.
